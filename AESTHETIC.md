# Aesthetic Statement

**Aesthetic:** Neo-Chinese style
**Link:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Chinese_style

## How I used CSS to show this style

I picked soft pink as my main color, with a little green mixed in, and a small red accent I only use once in a while. I kept every color low-saturation on purpose. Real Chinese traditional colors are rarely bright or loud, so I wanted mine to feel calm too.

I also gave each section its own color block instead of one flat background. Career Goals stays plain and quiet. Interests fades from pink into green. The whole Art section turns green. Food gets a warm tone with a red border, since red usually means good food and good luck in Chinese culture. This made the page feel like a set of rooms, not one long scroll.

For fonts, I paired Noto Serif SC for headings with Inter for body text. The serif feels traditional and a little formal, and the sans-serif keeps paragraphs easy to read. Old and new sitting side by side is basically the whole point of Neo-Chinese style.

I also drew a small lattice pattern with pure CSS as a divider between sections, no image needed. It's a nod to the wooden window screens in traditional Chinese buildings. It turns green right when you enter the Art section, so the color shift feels connected to the pattern instead of random.

Small circular photos sit next to a few headings too, like a lantern photo by Career Goals or a crane photo by Art. They're just quiet decoration, so they don't distract from the text.

## My JavaScript enhancement

I built a small gallery viewer. Each photo section shows one photo at a time with arrow buttons, looping back to the start. Clicking a photo opens a bigger popup with its own arrows, dots to jump around, and a close button. If JavaScript ever fails, the photos are still all there and scrollable by hand, so nothing important breaks.

## One accessibility decision

I made every focus outline green instead of pink. My palette is pink and rose, so a pink focus ring would basically disappear into the background. Green stands out no matter where you're tabbing, so keyboard users always know where they are.

## Medium is the message

If I put this same text on LinkedIn, it would sit in the same gray-and-white box as everyone else's profile. Same font, layout, same profile circle. LinkedIn isn't mine, so I can't change any of that. The words would say I like painting and cooking, but the page would look exactly like an accountant's page. The format wouldn't tell you I actually care about any of it.
 
That's the part of McLuhan I keep thinking about. He says the medium changes the scale of things, not just the container. A whisper and a shout through a speaker carry the same words, but the speaker changes what those words do. My website is the speaker I actually own. I get to pick the pink, the serif headings, the lantern and crane photos, the way Art shifts into green. None of that is required to tell you my major. But it's what makes the page feel like mine instead of a form I filled out.
